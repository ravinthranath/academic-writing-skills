# Levels of study and university standards

Use this file to calibrate any edit, draft or review to the writer's **level of study**. The same paragraph can be excellent first-year work and weak Master's work.

**Order of authority (the "university standard"):**
1. The user's own assessment brief, rubric or marking criteria.
2. The institution's handbook or regulations.
3. National level descriptors (section 3).
4. The generic level rules in this file.

National descriptors are thresholds and reference points, not marking schemes. The UK Quality Assurance Agency (QAA) says its classification descriptors are "not intended to be used as a standardised marking framework", and AAC&U says its VALUE rubrics are not for grading. Always tell the user that their institution's criteria come first.

## 1. Detect the level

Use explicit statements ("second-year essay", "MSc dissertation", "PhD chapter") or cues: word count, "viva", "capstone", "honors thesis", "Level 6", "EPQ". If the level is unclear and it changes the advice, ask one question: "What level and country is this for?"

## 2. Level selector

| Code | Who | Dominant move | Sources | Criticality | Independence and method | Originality | Typical long piece* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L3** Pre-university | A-level, IB, Access, foundation year, EPQ, US high school/dual enrolment | Explain and apply | Textbooks, set readings, a few credible articles | Weighs two sides; simple reasoned judgement | Teacher-directed | Not expected | 1,500–4,000-word coursework or EPQ |
| **UG1** First year | UK FHEQ 4, CertHE; US first-year composition / lower division | Describe accurately, explain, begin to analyse | Set reading plus some scholarly sources | Separates claim from evidence; basic evaluation | Structured tasks | Not expected | 1,500–2,500-word essays |
| **UG2** Intermediate | UK FHEQ 5, DipHE, Foundation degree, HND; US sophomore / associate | Analyse and compare approaches | Textbooks plus peer-reviewed articles beyond set lists | Evaluates which approach fits; states limits of own knowledge | Some initiative | Minimal | 2,500–4,000-word essays and reports |
| **UG3** Final year / honours | UK FHEQ 6; SCQF 9–10; EQF 6; AQF 7–8; US upper division, senior capstone, honors thesis | Evaluate and synthesise into a sustained argument | Current research, primary sources, reviews, reading beyond the list | Critiques assumptions and incomplete data; acknowledges uncertainty | Initiates and carries out a project; justifies method | "Some originality and creativity" (SCQF 10) | UK dissertation 8,000–12,000 words; US honors thesis often 30–60 pages |
| **M** Master's | UK FHEQ 7 (PGCert, PGDip, MA, MSc, MRes); SCQF 11; EQF 7; AQF 9; US master's | Critique the field and its methods; originality in applying knowledge | Forefront literature; structured or systematic review; methodological literature | Critiques methodologies; judges with incomplete data; positions own work in the debate | Self-directed; full design and ethics justification | Original application; new hypotheses where appropriate | UK dissertation about 12,000–20,000 words (MRes longer); US thesis often 40–80+ pages |
| **D** Doctoral | UK FHEQ 8 (PhD, DPhil, EdD, DBA); SCQF 12; EQF 8; AQF 10; US PhD | Create new knowledge and defend it | Substantial, comprehensive, critical command of the field | Synthesis and evaluation of new and complex ideas | Designs and adapts a substantial research process | Original contribution to knowledge of publishable quality | UK thesis up to about 80,000–100,000 words; US dissertation often 100–300 pages |

*Lengths vary by discipline and institution. Present them as "typical; your brief decides", never as rules.

## 3. What national descriptors expect (short anchors)

**UK FHEQ (QAA, 2024)**
- **Level 4:** communicate results "accurately and reliably, and with structured and coherent arguments"; evaluate and interpret data "in accordance with basic theories and concepts".
- **Level 5:** "critical understanding of the well-established principles"; evaluate the appropriateness of different approaches; "an understanding of the limits of their knowledge".
- **Level 6:** "devise and sustain arguments"; use "scholarly reviews and primary sources"; "critically evaluate arguments, assumptions, abstract concepts and data (that may be incomplete)"; appreciate "uncertainty, ambiguity and limits of knowledge".
- **Level 7:** critical awareness of current problems at "the forefront" of the field; "originality in the application of knowledge"; "evaluate methodologies and develop critiques of them"; "make sound judgements in the absence of complete data".
- **Level 8:** "the creation and interpretation of new knowledge… of a quality to satisfy peer review, extend the forefront of the discipline, and merit publication"; adjust the project "in the light of unforeseen problems".

**Europe (Dublin descriptors, QF-EHEA)**
- First cycle: knowledge supported by advanced textbooks, informed in part by the forefront; gather and interpret data to make judgements.
- Second cycle: originality in developing or applying ideas; integrate knowledge, handle complexity, judge with incomplete data; communicate to specialist and non-specialist audiences.
- Third cycle: mastery of research methods; contribution through original research, some of it worthy of refereed publication.

**Australia (AQF):** Bachelor (7) broad and coherent knowledge; Honours (8) advanced knowledge; Master's (9) specialised knowledge, critical understanding and independent research; Doctorate (10) significant and original contribution to knowledge.

**Canada (Ontario degree level expectations):** bachelor's graduates understand the limits of their knowledge; honours adds an appreciation of uncertainty and ambiguity; graduate levels add a critical awareness of current problems and new insights.

**US reference standards**
- WPA Outcomes Statement for First-Year Composition: rhetorical knowledge; critical thinking, reading and composing; processes; knowledge of conventions. Critical writers "separate assertion from evidence" and make "appropriately qualified" claims.
- AAC&U VALUE Written Communication rubric (Benchmark 1 → Milestones 2–3 → Capstone 4): context and purpose; content development; genre and disciplinary conventions; sources and evidence; syntax and mechanics.
- Lumina Degree Qualifications Profile: each degree level builds on the last; master's students create sustained, coherent arguments for general and specialist audiences.
- Council of Graduate Schools: a doctoral project should be original, substantial, significant and independently carried out, then defended orally.

## 4. Grade bands and what separates them

**UK honours (QAA 2024 descriptors, summarised)**

| Band | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| First (70+) | Knowledge well beyond what was taught; synthesises a broad range of research and primary sources; critical insight; some originality |
| 2:1 (60–69) | Breadth and depth with clear critical insight; sources critically evaluated, usually beyond the set range; coherent, substantiated argument |
| 2:2 (50–59) | Sound knowledge but sometimes more descriptive than critical; logical argument with evidence |
| Third (40–49) | Enough knowledge of terms and concepts; some evaluation and some consideration of other views |
| Fail (<40) | Inadequate knowledge; over-reliance on set sources; cannot critically evaluate others' arguments |

**UK taught Master's:** Distinction 70+, Merit 60–69, Pass 50–59, fail below 50. Typical Distinction work is methodologically sophisticated, intelligently argued, and shows some genuine originality or the ability to challenge received interpretations; 80+ work may be publishable. Many universities also require the dissertation itself to reach the band.

**UK doctorate:** pass/fail, not graded. Examined on the thesis and an oral examination (viva), usually with at least one external examiner. Outcomes include minor or major corrections.

**Australia:** commonly HD 85+, D 75–84, C 65–74, P 50–64 (some universities set each boundary five points lower). Credit is a good result.

**US:** no national grade descriptor; A–F rubrics are set by course or programme. As a rough heuristic only, VALUE Capstone 4 ≈ A-quality senior work, Milestone 3 ≈ B, Milestone 2 ≈ C.

**Never convert marks between countries** without saying it is approximate.

## 5. Per-level rules, common weaknesses and checklists

**L3 Pre-university**
- Rules: clear thesis; PEEL paragraphs (Point, Evidence, Explain, Link); define key terms; balanced two-sided discussion; reasoned conclusion; formal register.
- Weaknesses: narrating instead of answering; unsupported opinion; web sources of unknown authority.
- Check: Does every paragraph answer the question? Is each claim supported? Does the conclusion judge, not just summarise?

**UG1 First year**
- Rules: answer the command word; paraphrase and comment rather than quote; separate assertion from evidence; use scholarly sources, not only websites and slides; reference accurately; hedged but clear claims.
- Weaknesses: description instead of analysis; over-quotation and patchwriting; one source per paragraph; weak paragraph structure; inaccurate referencing.
- Check: Is my argument stated in the introduction? Does each paragraph have a point, evidence and my analysis? Is quotation kept to a minimum (a rough guide is under 10% of the text)? Are all sources referenced in the required style?

**UG2 Intermediate**
- Rules: compare competing theories or approaches and judge which fits the case; group sources by theme; state the limits of the evidence and of your analysis; apply concepts to new contexts.
- Weaknesses: listing views without deciding between them; boilerplate "strengths and weaknesses"; little reading beyond set texts.
- Check: Have I said which approach is more persuasive and why? Are sources grouped by theme? Have I acknowledged at least one limitation?

**UG3 Final year / honours / capstone**
- Rules: sustain one argument throughout; engage current research and primary sources beyond the reading list; critique assumptions and data; acknowledge uncertainty; in a dissertation, justify the question, method and ethics and frame findings against the literature.
- Weaknesses: literature review as an annotated list; a gap claimed but not evidenced; methods described but not justified; discussion repeating results; conclusions that overclaim.
- Check: Can I state my argument in two sentences? Does the literature review build towards my question? Is each method choice justified against alternatives? Have I discussed limitations and implications?

**M Master's**
- Rules: position the work at the forefront; critique methodologies, not only findings; make and defend judgements with incomplete data; show originality in applying knowledge; give an explicit design rationale (paradigm, sampling, analysis, ethics); add reflexivity where the discipline expects it; write for specialist and non-specialist readers.
- Weaknesses: undergraduate breadth without critical depth; reviewing sources instead of debates; a methodology chapter that summarises textbooks without justification; no clear contribution or implication; weak links between question, method and findings.
- Check: Is the debate, not the topic, the unit of my review? Have I critiqued the key methods in my field? Is my design justified and are its limits owned? What is new about how I apply knowledge? Is there a clear "so what" for both practitioners and academics?

**D Doctoral**
- Rules: state the original contribution explicitly and early, and return to it in each chapter and the conclusion; keep one argument across chapters (or an integrating narrative across papers); show command of a substantial body of knowledge; defend design decisions, including adaptations; write chapters of publishable quality; prepare to defend every claim orally.
- Weaknesses: a buried or unclear contribution; a literature review disconnected from empirical chapters; "thesis as report"; overclaiming generalisability; inconsistent terminology across chapters; missing implications for theory, method and practice.
- Check: Can I state my contribution in one paragraph (theory, method, data or application)? Does every chapter advance the argument? What are the three hardest viva or defence questions, and does the thesis answer them? Are limitations and future work specific?

## 6. Bloom, SOLO and command words

**Bloom's revised taxonomy by level (dominant, not exclusive):** remember/understand (L3–UG1) → apply/analyse (UG1–UG2) → evaluate (UG3) → create (M: original application; D: new knowledge).

**SOLO taxonomy (Biggs and Collis)**

| SOLO level | Looks like | Verbs | Typical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prestructural | Misses the point | — | Fail at any level |
| Unistructural | One relevant aspect | identify, name, define | Weak UG1 |
| Multistructural | Several aspects, unconnected | describe, list, classify | UG1–UG2; the "descriptive 2:2" trap |
| Relational | Aspects integrated into a structure | analyse, explain, compare, relate, apply | UG3 / 2:1 |
| Extended abstract | Goes beyond what was given | theorise, hypothesise, generalise, evaluate, create | First, Distinction, doctoral |

**Command words**
- **Describe / outline:** what, not why; concise; no evaluation unless asked.
- **Explain:** causes and mechanisms ("because", "therefore").
- **Analyse:** break into parts, show relationships, identify assumptions.
- **Compare / contrast:** point by point; similarities and differences; say which differences matter.
- **Discuss:** several perspectives, weighed, ending in a reasoned position (not a neutral survey).
- **Evaluate / assess:** judge against explicit criteria, then conclude.
- **Critically evaluate / analyse:** as above, plus interrogate evidence quality, methods, assumptions and limits; at M and D level, critique methodologies.
- **Justify:** reasons and evidence for a choice, including why alternatives were rejected.
- **To what extent:** a graded judgement ("largely", "only partly"), not yes or no.
- **Synthesise:** combine sources into a new account organised by theme or argument.

## 7. The same paragraph at four levels

Topic: *Does remote work increase productivity?* "(Author, Year)" marks placeholders; never invent real citations.

**First-year undergraduate (describe, begin to explain)**
> Remote work has become more common since 2020, and many studies have examined whether it affects productivity. Some research suggests that employees working from home are more productive because they avoid commuting and face fewer interruptions (Author, Year). However, other studies report that remote workers can feel isolated, which may reduce their performance (Author, Year). This suggests that the effect of remote work on productivity is not simple and may depend on the individual worker.

Shows accurate summary, two sides and a tentative judgement. Lacks an explanation of why the studies differ.

**Final-year undergraduate (evaluate and synthesise)**
> Evidence on remote work and productivity is mixed, but the disagreement is partly methodological. Studies reporting gains tend to rely on firm-level output measures in routine, individually measurable tasks (Author, Year; Author, Year), whereas those reporting losses more often use self-report measures in collaborative roles (Author, Year). Read together, these findings suggest that task interdependence, rather than location itself, moderates the effect. This interpretation remains provisional, since few studies control for selection into remote work and much post-2020 evidence was produced under atypical conditions.

Shows thematic synthesis, critique of measures, a reasoned position and acknowledged limits.

**Master's (critique methods; original application)**
> The productivity debate has been framed largely as a contest between output-based and perception-based measures, yet both traditions share an unexamined assumption: that productivity is an individual attribute. Quasi-experimental studies offer stronger causal leverage than cross-sectional surveys (Author, Year), but their settings, typically call centres and coding tasks, limit transfer to knowledge work in which value is co-produced. Drawing on sociotechnical systems theory (Author, Year), this dissertation reframes productivity as a property of team workflows and therefore measures it through coordination costs rather than individual output. This choice sacrifices comparability with prior work in exchange for construct validity in collaborative settings, a trade-off discussed in Chapter 3.

Shows critique of methods and assumptions, positioning in the debate, original application of theory and a justified design trade-off.

**Doctoral (original contribution, positioned and bounded)**
> This thesis argues that research on remote-work productivity has reached an impasse because it treats location as the treatment variable, when the theoretically relevant mechanism is the redistribution of coordination work. Building on, but departing from, sociotechnical accounts (Author, Year), it develops and tests a coordination-load model using matched digital-trace and performance data from three organisations (Chapters 4–6). The contribution is threefold: conceptually, it relocates productivity from the individual to the workflow; methodologically, it shows how trace data can measure coordination without self-report; and empirically, it identifies the conditions under which remote arrangements raise or lower team output. These claims are bounded by the organisations' sectors and by the post-2020 period, as discussed in Chapter 7.

Shows an explicit three-part contribution, independent design, scholarly positioning and bounded claims. (Many disciplines accept "I" in a doctoral thesis; follow the field.)

## 8. Reviewing against the level

In review mode, report against six dials, using the band language in section 4:

1. **Knowledge depth:** accurate, current, appropriate to the level?
2. **Criticality:** descriptive, analytical, evaluative or original?
3. **Source use:** type, range, currency, synthesis versus listing?
4. **Independence and method:** choices made and justified?
5. **Originality:** what is new, if the level expects it?
6. **Communication:** structure, register, conventions, audience.

Where possible, say the level at which the work currently reads (for example, "reads as a solid 2:1 / Merit-level analysis; to reach Distinction, critique the methods of the two key studies") and give the two or three changes that would move it up a band.

**Integrity:** humanizing or editing must never raise the apparent level by inventing sources, data or claims. Higher-level work comes from the writer's own reading and analysis; the skill can point out where it is needed and flag gaps.
