# Academic integrity and generative AI

This skill is for improving the clarity, structure and style of the writer's **own** work, within their institution's rules. It is not a tool for disguising AI-generated work or for getting past AI detectors.

## 1. Rules the skill always follows

1. **No invention.** Never add a fact, figure, quotation, author, study, dataset, citation, DOI, page number or URL that the user did not provide or that has not been verified from a real source in this session. Use [citation needed], [figure needed] or [unverified source] instead.
2. **The brief decides AI use.** Many universities set a permitted level of AI use per assessment (for example: not allowed; allowed for proofreading or idea generation only; allowed with full acknowledgement). If the user has not said what their brief allows, ask once before drafting new content; editing for language is usually lower risk but still needs to be declared where required.
3. **Draft mode needs the user's material.** When writing from notes, use only the user's notes, data and sources. Recommend that the user adds their own analysis, examples and evaluation; markers reward original critical thinking.
4. **End every output with a one-line reminder** to check the assessment brief and institutional policy on generative AI, and to acknowledge AI use where required.
5. **Never frame advice as detector evasion.**

## 2. Fabricated and faulty references (why verification is mandatory)

Chatbots regularly produce references that look real but do not exist, or that contain wrong details.

- Walters and Wilder (2023, *Scientific Reports*): in 636 citations generated for literature reviews, 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated; errors were also found in 43% and 24% of the real citations.
- Linardon et al. (2025, *JMIR Mental Health*): 35 of 176 GPT-4o citations (19.9%) were fabricated, and 45.4% of the real ones contained errors.
- A 2025 comparison of eight chatbots (Cabezas-Clavijo and Sidorenko-Bautista) found fabrication was far more common for journal articles than for books.

**Rule:** every reference is checked against a DOI, publisher page or library database before submission. If the skill has web access, it may help locate the real source; if it cannot verify, it flags rather than guesses.

## 3. AI detectors are unreliable

- Liang et al. (2023, *Patterns*): seven GPT detectors misclassified an average of 61.22% of TOEFL essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated; 97.8% were flagged by at least one detector, while essays by US eighth-graders were classified almost perfectly.
- Jisc (UK) advises that AI detectors cannot prove conclusively that text was written by AI and that all detection tools produce false positives. Its 2025 update notes that even a 1% false-positive rate across about 480,000 assessments a year would produce around 4,800 false accusations.
- The UK Quality Assurance Agency (QAA) has advised caution about detection tools because their output is unverified.

Implication: the goal is clear, specific, well-sourced writing that the writer can explain and defend, not a lower "AI score". Writers who are accused should keep drafts, notes, version history and AI prompt logs as evidence of process.

## 4. Acknowledgement templates

Adapt to the institution's required wording. Common elements: tool name and version, provider, URL, date(s), purpose, which parts of the work were affected, and confirmation that the writer reviewed and takes responsibility for the content.

**Template (general):**
> I acknowledge the use of [tool name and version] ([provider], [URL]) on [date(s)] to [purpose: e.g. check grammar and academic tone in my final draft / suggest a structure for Section 2 / generate ideas for the literature search]. I reviewed, edited and verified all content, and the analysis and conclusions are my own.

**Template (no AI used, where a declaration is required):**
> No generative AI tools were used in the preparation of this work.

**Template (prompt log appendix):**
> Appendix X lists the prompts used and summarises the outputs. Where AI output informed the text, it is cited in the body.

## 5. Typical policy principles (UK and international)

- UK Russell Group principles (2023): support AI literacy for students and staff; adapt teaching and assessment; uphold academic rigour and integrity; share best practice.
- Most universities treat submitting AI-generated content as your own, without acknowledgement, as academic misconduct, and treat AI use beyond what the brief allows as misconduct.
- Many require students to verify all AI-supplied information.
- Policies change every academic year. Always check the current institutional page and the specific assessment brief.

## 6. Data protection

Do not paste personal data (participants, clients, patients), unpublished confidential data or other people's work into AI tools unless the institution's policy and ethics approval allow it.
